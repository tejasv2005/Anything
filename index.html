Season 01

The Origin — Before the Cup Was Invented

The Legends

How Coffee Came to Exist

🐐

Ethiopia · The Kaldi Legend (~850 AD)

A goat herder named Kaldi in the Kaffa region of Ethiopia noticed his goats dancing at night, refusing to sleep after eating red berries from a certain tree. He brought the berries to a local monastery. A monk threw them into a fire in disapproval — and the first-ever coffee aroma filled the room. The monks brewed the roasted seeds in water and stayed alert through long evening prayers. This story is almost certainly mythology — but its geography is true. Ethiopia is the genetic home of Coffea arabica. The plant is indigenous there. Coffee was not invented anywhere else.

🕌

Yemen · The First Cultivation (15th Century)

Sufi monks in Yemen were the first to deliberately cultivate and brew coffee into a drink — "qahwa" — used during long night vigils and prayers. The port city of Mocha (Al-Mokha) became the world's first coffee export hub. Yemenis were so protective of their monopoly that coffee was exported only in roasted or boiled form — to prevent germination. For almost 200 years, Yemen controlled the entire global supply. Every cup of coffee in the Ottoman Empire, every coffee house in Cairo or Constantinople, ran on Yemeni beans.

🧎

India · Baba Budan's Seven Seeds (1670)

A Sufi saint from Karnataka — Hazrat Baba Budan — was returning from his Hajj pilgrimage when he stopped at the port of Mocha. Captivated by qahwa, he smuggled seven raw coffee seeds out of Yemen, hiding them in his beard. Exporting unroasted coffee was illegal under Yemeni law. He planted those seeds on the Chandragiri Hills of Chikkamagaluru, Karnataka — now known as Baba Budan Giri. From seven seeds grew India's entire coffee industry: 2.8 lakh metric tonnes produced in Karnataka alone in 2025-26. His tomb in those hills is today a syncretic pilgrimage site — sacred to both Hindus and Muslims.

☕

Europe · The Coffeehouses (17th Century)

Coffee arrived in Europe via Ottoman trade routes. The first European coffeehouse opened in Venice in 1645. By 1700, London had over 2,000 coffeehouses — called "penny universities" because for the price of a penny (the cost of admission, including coffee), any man could sit and engage with intellectuals, merchants, politicians, and philosophers. Lloyd's of London — the world's largest insurance market — started as a coffeehouse. The London Stock Exchange began in a coffeehouse. Modern capitalism was, in a very real sense, brewed in coffee.

"Baba Budan didn't just bring coffee to India. He committed an act of industrial espionage that birthed an entire subcontinent's relationship with the most consumed psychoactive substance on earth."
The Plant

Coffea — The Species Behind the Cup

🌿

Coffea Arabica

70% of global coffee. Higher altitude (800–2200m). Lower caffeine, higher acidity, more complex flavour. Susceptible to disease and weather. Ethiopian origin. Varieties: Bourbon, Typica, Heirloom, Gesha/Geisha, SL28, Caturra, Mundo Novo. This is specialty coffee's domain.

💪

Coffea Canephora (Robusta)

30% of global coffee. Lower altitude, more robust, higher caffeine (2x Arabica), earthy, harsh, rubbery when low-quality. Resilient. Used in commercial blends, Italian espresso blends, instant coffee. Vietnamese Robusta can be specialty-grade. Harder to brew well black — often served with sweetened condensed milk.

🌸

Coffea Liberica

Very rare. Grown in West Africa, Philippines, Malaysia. Woody, floral, smoky, almost like jackfruit. Used in some Southeast Asian blends. Rarely found outside its origin regions. Considered an acquired taste even by seasoned drinkers.

🔬

Coffea Excelsa

Often classified under Liberica. Grown in Southeast Asia. Tart, fruity, complex. Used in blends for depth. Gaining niche interest in the specialty world for its uniqueness. Very limited global production.

The Coffee Cherry

Coffee "beans" are not beans at all. They are the seeds of a fruit — the coffee cherry. Understanding the cherry is understanding why processing decisions matter so profoundly for taste.

🍒

Outer Skin (Exocarp)

The red (or yellow, orange, purple) outer skin. Removed during processing. Color at harvest determines ripeness. Only fully red cherries should be picked for specialty — even one underripe cherry in a batch degrades the cup.

🫐

Pulp (Mesocarp)

Sweet, fruit-forward, sugary layer directly beneath the skin. In natural processing, this pulp ferments against the bean and transfers its fruity sweetness into the final cup. In washed processing, it is removed entirely by machine.

🔮

Mucilage

A sticky, gummy layer between pulp and parchment. Crucial to honey processing — how much mucilage is left on the bean determines whether the honey is yellow, red, or black. Contains complex sugars that ferment into flavour compounds.

📜

Parchment + Silver Skin + Bean

The parchment (endocarp) protects the actual green bean during drying. Inside it: the silver skin (chaff), which burns off during roasting. And finally, the two flat-sided seeds — the green coffee beans — each with its own distinct cell structure that will determine how it roasts.

The Waves

Four Waves — Coffee's Cultural Evolution

1

First Wave

1800s–1960s

Coffee as commodity. Mass production. The goal: caffeine delivery, not flavour. Dark roasts to hide defects. Canned, pre-ground, instant. Shelf life over taste.

Folgers · Maxwell House · Nescafé · Bru (India) · Tata Coffee
2

Second Wave

1966–2000s

Coffee as experience and lifestyle. Origin awareness begins. Espresso culture goes mainstream in the West. Flavored drinks, dairy-heavy, dark Italian roasts. Consumer pays premium for atmosphere, not bean quality.

Starbucks · Peet's · Costa · Café Coffee Day (India) · Barista Lavazza
3

Third Wave

2000s–2020s

Coffee as craft and terroir. Origin transparency. Light roasts to showcase bean character. Direct trade. Single origins. The barista as skilled professional. The cup scored scientifically. WBC becomes major platform.

Blue Bottle · Stumptown · Intelligentsia · Counter Culture · Blue Tokai · Third Wave Coffee · Subko · Corridor Seven (India)
4

Fourth Wave (Emerging)

2020s–

Coffee as science. Anaerobic fermentation. Precision extraction. Water chemistry as variable. AI-assisted roasting. Lab-grade measurement at home (refractometers, Bluetooth scales, pressure profiling). Experimental processing.

Onyx Coffee Lab · Proud Mary · Decent Espresso · Acaia · Weber Workshops

Season 02

Cherry to Green Bean — The Processing Methods

Harvesting First

How Cherries Are Picked — It Changes Everything

✋

Selective Hand Picking

Pickers go through each tree multiple times (2–3 passes per season) selecting only fully ripe red cherries. Labour-intensive, expensive, but produces the most consistent quality. Standard in specialty coffee and high-altitude farms. What you pay a premium for.

Used in: Ethiopia · Colombia · Panama · India (specialty estates)
👐

Strip Picking

All cherries — ripe, unripe, overripe — stripped from branches in one pass. Fast and cheap. Results in uneven quality. Green, red, and black cherries all end up together, creating defects in the final cup. Standard in large commodity farms.

Used in: Brazil commodity · commercial farms globally
🚜

Machine Harvesting

Large mechanical harvesters shake tree trunks or branches. Only feasible on flat terrain (Brazil's vast Cerrado plains). Extremely efficient for commodity scale — devastating for quality. Ripe and unripe together. The opposite philosophy from specialty.

Used in: Brazil large-scale farms
📊

Density Sorting (Post-Harvest)

Cherries float in water — fully ripe (denser) sink, defective or unripe ones float. Called "flotation sorting." Removes a significant proportion of defects before processing begins. A simple, ancient technique that dramatically improves floor quality even before any processing decision is made.

Used in: most specialty operations as a quality gate
The Processing Methods

Six Ways to Turn a Cherry into a Green Bean

Natural / Dry Process
Oldest Method

Whole cherries — skin, pulp, mucilage and all — are spread on raised drying beds or patios and left to dry in the sun for 3–6 weeks. The bean sits inside the fermenting, drying fruit the entire time, absorbing its sugars and fruit compounds through the cell wall.

Flavour result: Heavy, wine-like, fruity, boozy, blueberry, tropical fruit, full body. Inconsistent by nature — prone to over-fermentation if not turned constantly.

Origin: Ethiopia, Yemen → now global
Risk: Rot and defects if rain or humidity interrupts drying
Famous for: Ethiopian naturals (Yirgacheffe, Sidama), Brazilian naturals
Washed / Wet Process
Cleanest Cup

Skin and pulp are mechanically removed immediately after harvest. The bean (still in parchment, covered in mucilage) is then soaked in fermentation tanks for 12–72 hours. Fermentation breaks down the remaining mucilage enzymatically. The beans are then washed clean with fresh water and dried.

Flavour result: Clean, bright, high acidity, fruit clarity, transparent — you taste the bean's intrinsic character most directly. The terroir of origin is most legible.

Origin: Developed in Central America, perfected in East Africa
Famous for: Kenyan washed, Yirgacheffe washed, Colombian
Honey Process
Spectrum of Styles

A hybrid between natural and washed. Skin is removed (like washed) but varying amounts of mucilage are deliberately left on the bean (like natural), which then dries on the parchment. How much mucilage is retained determines the "colour" of the honey:

Yellow Honey: 75–90% mucilage removed, quick drying. Subtle sweetness, clean.

Red Honey: 50–75% removed, slower drying. More fruity, caramel notes.

Black Honey: Only 10–25% removed, very slow drying (30+ days). Closest to natural. Rich, complex, jammy, intense sweetness.

Origin: Costa Rica (pioneered), now Central America-wide
Risk: Requires precise weather control to avoid fermentation runaway
Anaerobic Fermentation
4th Wave / Experimental

Cherries or depulped beans are sealed in airtight tanks with no oxygen. CO₂ builds up from natural fermentation, creating a unique pressure and chemical environment. Fermentation time: 24–200+ hours depending on desired profile. Produces radically unique, often polarising flavours — lactic acid notes, wine, funk, tropical intensity.

Flavour result: Wild, intense, fermented-fruity, often described as "funky" or "boozy." Designed to stand out, not blend in.

Famous for: Sasa Sestic's 2015 WBC-winning coffee, now widespread in specialty
Risk: Easy to go from "complex" to "defective" — precision required
Wet-Hulled / Giling Basah
Uniquely Indonesian

Specific to Indonesia (Sumatra, Sulawesi, Flores). Cherries are partially washed, then the parchment is removed while the bean still has very high moisture content (35–50%). This exposed, naked bean then continues drying. The process creates distinctive earthy, herbal, low-acid, full-body flavours entirely unlike any other origin.

Flavour result: Earthy, musty (in a good way), cedar, tobacco, low acidity, heavy body. Very distinctive. Often used in espresso blends for depth.

Why this method: High humidity in Indonesia makes full drying impractical. Giling Basah developed as a practical solution.
Monsooned / Malabar
India's Gift

A uniquely Indian process specific to the Malabar Coast (Karnataka/Kerala). Green beans are stored in open warehouses during the monsoon season (June–September) and exposed to warm, moist monsoon winds for 12–16 weeks. The beans swell, turn pale golden/yellow, lose much of their acidity, and develop an entirely unique character.

Flavour result: Near-zero acidity, extremely full body, pungent, spicy, musty, almost like aged wine. Loves milk. Critical component of classic South Indian filter coffee blends.

GI tag: Monsooned Malabar has an official Geographical Indication (GI) tag
Key estate regions: Chikkamagaluru, Coorg, Wayanad

Season 03

The Roast — Chemistry, Heat, and the Maillard Secret

The Science

What Actually Happens Inside the Drum

A green coffee bean is grassy, dense, and largely flavourless. Put it through 7–14 minutes of precisely controlled heat and it becomes one of the most chemically complex foods on earth — with over 1,000 volatile aromatic compounds. Here is the journey:

Phase 1 · Drying (0–4 min · 0–160°C)

Moisture Evaporation

Green beans contain 8–12% moisture. The first phase drives this out. The drum heats the beans — they feel and look almost the same, still green, turning slightly yellow. No aroma yet. The roaster charges the drum and watches bean temperature. Endothermic phase — beans are absorbing heat, not releasing it.

Phase 2 · Maillard Reaction (~150–170°C)

The Browning Begins

The Maillard reaction is the same chemical process that browns bread, sears steak, and caramelises onions. Reducing sugars react with amino acids under heat. Hundreds of new flavour and aroma compounds are created. The beans turn from yellow to tan to brown. The roastery fills with aroma. This phase determines most of the cup's complexity — and where roasters spend the most attention.

Phase 3 · First Crack (~196°C)

The Bean Expands and Pops

As internal cell pressure builds from steam and CO₂, the beans crack open audibly — a sharp, popcorn-like sound. First crack marks the transition from endothermic to exothermic. This is technically the minimum for drinkable coffee — and where most specialty light roasts stop. Sugars are caramelising. Acidity is at its highest. Sweetness is developing. The origin character of the bean is most legible here.

Phase 4 · Development Time (Post-First Crack)

Where the Roaster Makes Decisions

After first crack, the roaster has a short window — typically 1–3 minutes — to decide how much "development" to give the coffee. More development = more body, less acidity, more bittersweet complexity. Less development = more brightness, higher acidity, fruit-forward, origin clarity. This is the single most consequential decision in roasting. Temperature, time, and airflow all matter here.

Phase 5 · Second Crack (~225°C)

Dark Roast Territory — Oils Emerge

A second, quieter crack as cell walls fracture further. Oils migrate to the bean surface (visible shine). Origin character is now largely obliterated — all coffees begin to taste more like "roast" than like where they came from. Sugars are burning. Acidity collapses. Body becomes heavier, more bitter. This is Italian espresso territory and commercial dark roast. Not worse — just a different philosophy.

Roast Levels

The Spectrum from Light to Dark

196°C

Light Roast

Just after first crack ends. Dry, matte surface. No oils visible. Highest acidity. Fruit-forward, floral, tea-like. Origin is paramount — a poor bean cannot hide. Best brewed as pour over, filter, AeroPress. Specialty's domain. Very forgiving of water quality, very unforgiving of low-quality beans.

Examples: Ethiopian Yirgacheffe · Kenyan AA · Panamanian Gesha
210°C

Medium Roast

Between first and second crack. No surface oils. Balance of acidity and body. Caramel sweetness. Fruit notes persist but mellow. Most versatile — works for espresso, pour over, drip, AeroPress. The default for specialty cafes trying to serve multiple brew methods without rebelling.

Examples: Colombian · Brazilian · Costa Rican · most Indian specialty
225°C

Medium-Dark Roast

Approaching second crack. Some surface oils. Dark chocolate, caramel, roast notes dominate. Acidity reduced. Body increases. Classic Italian espresso range. Good for milky drinks — holds up to steamed milk without disappearing. French press territory.

Examples: Viennese · Full City · Italian-style blends
230°C+

Dark Roast

Into second crack and beyond. Heavy surface oils (shiny beans). Bitter, smoky, charcoal, tobacco, bittersweet chocolate. Origin character essentially gone — you taste the roaster's hand, not the farm. Forgiving of bad beans. Classic café au lait, commercial espresso blends, French roast.

Examples: French Roast · Italian Roast · Espresso blends (commercial)
Post-Roast — What Happens Next

The Rest Period, Degassing, and Storage

Roasting doesn't end when the beans leave the drum. A roasted coffee bean is alive with CO₂ — the gas produced during the roasting process. This CO₂ needs to escape (degas) before the coffee is ready to brew. This is why properly roasted coffee bags have one-way valves — CO₂ can escape out, oxygen cannot get in.

🫧

Degassing Period

Light roasts: 5–10 days before peak. Medium: 3–7 days. Dark: 1–3 days (degasses faster). Using coffee too soon after roasting creates over-gassed espresso — uneven extraction, large uneven crema, unstable pressure. Barista shops rest their espresso beans for 5–14 days post-roast before serving.

📦

Storage Rules

The enemies of roasted coffee: oxygen, light, heat, moisture. Store in: airtight container with one-way valve, away from sunlight, at room temperature. Do not refrigerate — condensation when you take the container out ruins beans. Freeze only if unopened and sealed airtight for long-term storage (whole beans only).

⏳

Peak Window & Freshness

Specialty coffee is at its best 7–21 days post-roast for espresso, 5–14 days for filter. After 4–6 weeks: flavour begins degrading noticeably. After 3 months: stale. This is the fundamental problem with supermarket coffee — "best before" dates can be 18 months from roast date. By the time it reaches you, it is already old.

Season 04

The Grind — Why the Mill Matters More Than the Machine

Grinder Types

Blade vs Burr — Not Even a Real Debate

❌ Blade Grinder — Do Not Use for Specialty Coffee

A blade grinder chops coffee randomly, like a blender. The result is a mix of particle sizes — some powder-fine, some boulder-large — that extract at wildly different rates in the same brew. Over-extraction and under-extraction in the same cup. The friction also generates heat, which damages volatile aromatics. There is no setting for coarse or fine — just "how long you hold the button." This is not grinding. This is destroying coffee.

Burr grinders use two abrasive surfaces (burrs) to crush beans into a consistent particle size. The gap between the burrs determines the grind size. All quality grinding — home or commercial — uses burrs.

💿

Flat Burr Grinders

Two parallel disc-shaped burrs facing each other. Coffee is forced through the gap and exits sideways. Produces highly uniform, consistent particle sizes. Creates a "bimodal" distribution — excellent clarity and brightness in the cup. The EK43 (Mahlkönig) is the most famous flat burr grinder in the world. Tends to produce cleaner, brighter, more analytical cups. Preferred for filter/pour-over in professional settings.

Notable: Mahlkönig EK43 · EK43S · Mythos One/Two · Weber EG-1 · Lagom P64
🔺

Conical Burr Grinders

A cone-shaped inner burr spins inside a ring-shaped outer burr. Coffee moves down through the gap by gravity. Produces a slightly more varied particle distribution — creates a fuller body and more complex sweetness. Generally more forgiving for espresso. Runs cooler and quieter than flat burrs. The Comandante C40 (hand grinder) is the most celebrated conical burr in the world.

Notable: Comandante C40 · Niche Zero · 1Zpresso JX-Pro · Baratza Encore · Fellow Ode Gen 2

9 BAR

The Espresso Pressure Standard

The SCA (Specialty Coffee Association) standard for espresso extraction is 9 bars of pump pressure at the group head. This was established after decades of research — at 9 bars, water extracts the right balance of compounds from a correctly ground, dosed, and tamped puck. Many cheap home machines advertise 15 or 19 bars. This is not better — it is worse. Marketing pressure spec. Actual quality espresso is pulled at 9 bars. Some modern machines (Decent DE1, La Marzocco machines with pressure profiling) actually begin at lower pressure (2–4 bars pre-infusion), then ramp up to 9 bars. This pre-infusion stage gently saturates the puck before full extraction — reducing channelling and improving consistency.

1:2

The Espresso Ratio

For every gram of ground coffee going into the portafilter, approximately 2 grams of espresso should come out. A 18g dose should yield approximately 36g of liquid espresso. This is the most used specialty benchmark. Variations: Ristretto (1:1 to 1:1.5) — shorter, sweeter, more concentrated. Lungo (1:3 to 1:4) — longer, more bitter, more water. Each produces a dramatically different cup from the same beans on the same machine.

94°C

Water Temperature

SCA standard: 91–94°C. Darker roasts extract better at lower temperatures (88–90°C) — they're more soluble. Lighter roasts need higher heat (92–96°C) to properly extract. Water off the boil (100°C) creates bitter over-extraction. Cold water creates sour under-extraction. The science: solubility of coffee's flavour compounds peaks at different temperatures — acids extract at lower temps, bitterness at higher. Dialling temperature is a serious extraction variable in specialty.

The Great Comparison

Espresso vs Pour Over vs French Press

☕

Espresso

Philosophy: Concentration and intensity. Extract the maximum in minimum time using pressure.

What you need: Espresso machine (9-bar), quality burr grinder capable of fine espresso grind, scale, tamper. All four are non-negotiable.

Learning curve: Very high. Every variable affects outcome dramatically. Takes months to dial in properly.

Best for: Black espresso shots, milk-based drinks, concentrated coffee culture. The base of every café drink.

Worst for: Casual, fuss-free mornings. Appreciation of subtle origin notes.

🫗

Pour Over (V60 / Chemex)

Philosophy: Clarity and origin expression. Let gravity and filter do the work. The barista controls every variable manually.

What you need: Dripper, gooseneck kettle, scale, timer, good grinder, filters. Relatively affordable entry.

Learning curve: Medium. Technique (pour pattern, timing, bloom) matters. But forgiving enough to start immediately.

Best for: Single-origin, light roasts, appreciating coffee as a flavour experience. The intellectual's brew method.

Worst for: Fast, effortless mornings. Milk-based drinks.

🏺

French Press

Philosophy: Full immersion, full body. No paper filter — oils and fines remain in the cup. The most democratic brewing method.

What you need: French press, coarse grinder, hot water, timer. The most affordable setup that still produces genuine quality.

Learning curve: Very low. Steep, press, pour. Forgiving of almost everything.

Best for: Rich, full-bodied coffee. Dark roasts. Weekend mornings. Someone entering specialty from a position of ease.

Worst for: Origin clarity, brightness, subtle aromatics. Also produces sediment.

Cold Coffee — The Full Spectrum

🧊

Cold Brew (Immersion)

Extra coarse grounds steeped in cold or room-temperature water for 12–24 hours. Filtered through paper or cloth. Produces a concentrate (1:8) diluted before serving. Extremely low acidity. Sweet, smooth, heavy. Mellow. Works best with medium-dark to dark roasts. Light roasts can taste flat and underwhelming in cold brew.

💧

Cold Drip / Kyoto Tower

Cold water drips, drop by drop, through a bed of coffee at room temperature. Takes 4–8 hours. Produces a cleaner, brighter cup than immersion cold brew — more like a cold pour over. Equipment is a Japanese-style glass tower. Spectacular to watch. Intensely labour-focused. Clarity and complexity together.

⚡

Flash-Brewed / Japanese Iced

Hot coffee brewed directly onto ice. V60 or AeroPress recipe adjusted: replace half the brewing water with ice in the cup, use hotter and more concentrated brew water. The rapid chilling locks in aromatics that cold brew loses. Bright, floral, transparent. The best method for appreciating a specialty light roast cold.

Season 06

Coffee Through Cultures — Five Countries, Five Philosophies

🇮🇹

Italian — The Church of Espresso

In Italy, coffee is not a beverage. It is a ritual with rules. Espresso — 25–30ml, pulled in under 30 seconds, consumed standing at the bar in under 2 minutes. Cappuccino is a morning drink only — never after 11am, never after a meal. Ordering a cappuccino after lunch marks you as a tourist immediately. The bar is a social institution: you pay before you order, slide your receipt across, exchange three sentences with the barista, drink, leave. The entire interaction is 4 minutes. Italian espresso blends favour Robusta for crema and body, using dark roasts. The "taste of roast" is considered a feature, not a defect. Lavazza, illy, Kimbo, Segafredo — household names.

🇯🇵

Japanese — Precision as Devotion

Japan's coffee culture is built around the Kissaten — the traditional Japanese coffee house, dating from the early 1900s. Quiet, contemplative, almost ceremonial. Japanese baristas are famous for their obsessive precision: each pour measured, each movement deliberate, each second timed. The V60 dripper itself is Japanese (Hario). Siphon/vacuum brewing is deeply embedded in Japanese café culture — theatrical, precise, beautiful. Kono, Kalita Wave, and Hario are Japanese tools that shaped the global pour-over movement. Japanese palate prefers clean, clarity, delicacy. No shouting. No rush. Your coffee is 10 minutes of stillness.

🇺🇸

American — Volume, Then Craft

America invented first-wave commodity coffee culture (Maxwell House, Folgers). Then it invented the third wave. This is not ironic — it is classic American overcorrection at scale. Drip coffee (auto-drip machines) remains the largest consumer format. But the third wave explosion produced the world's most influential specialty roasters: Blue Bottle, Stumptown, Intelligentsia, Counter Culture, Onyx, Heart, Sightglass. The American specialty scene is now scientifically rigorous, culturally diverse, and export-influential. Cold brew is an American commercial invention. The Frappuccino is American. America simultaneously has the worst and best coffee culture on earth.

🇫🇷

French — Dark, Philosophical, Dairy-Heavy

France's café culture is inseparable from its intellectual history — Voltaire reportedly consumed 40–50 cups a day, and the Revolution was planned in the coffeehouses of Paris. French roast is very dark, which compensates for historically lower-quality beans by adding roast character. Café au lait (equal parts brewed coffee and hot milk) is the canonical morning drink. French press is named for France but actually more of a European pan-tradition. France's specialty scene is smaller than the UK/Scandinavia/US but growing rapidly, particularly in Paris.

🇮🇳

Indian — Filter, Chicory, and Now the Third Wave

South Indian filter coffee (Kaapi) is one of the world's most distinct coffee traditions. A metal davara-tumbler set. A brass filter. Decoction (concentrated brew) made from dark-roasted Arabica + Robusta + chicory (20–40% chicory added for body and bitterness). Boiling hot milk, frothed by pouring between tumbler and davara from height. Served at 65–70°C. Sweet. Creamy. Non-negotiable in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala homes. This is India's first-wave heritage. And now: Blue Tokai, Third Wave Coffee, Subko, Corridor Seven, Filter Story — a full specialty third wave is rising, anchored by Araku Valley's tribal cooperatives, Coorg's estates, and Chikkamagaluru's legacy. India is the 7th largest coffee producer on earth and is only now learning to drink its own best beans.

🌍

Ethiopian — Coffee's Ancestral Home

In Ethiopia, the Coffee Ceremony (Buna) is the highest form of hospitality. Green beans are washed, then roasted in a pan over charcoal right in front of guests — the aroma of roasting fills the room. Ground in a wooden mortar. Brewed three times from the same grounds. First cup: "Abol" (strongest, for adults). Second: "Tona." Third: "Baraka" (blessing). The ceremony takes 1–2 hours. Sharing it is sharing your life. In Ethiopia, 60% of the coffee produced is consumed domestically — a country that lives inside its own origin story. Every specialty cup of Ethiopian coffee in the world traces back to here.

Tasting Coffee — Cupping, the Flavour Wheel, and Building a Palate

Cupping Protocol

How the Industry Tastes Coffee — The SCA Standard

Cupping is the standardised method for evaluating coffee quality, used by Q Graders, roasters, buyers, and baristas globally. The SCA (Specialty Coffee Association) defines the exact protocol:

⚖️

The Setup

Ratio: 8.25g of coffee per 150ml of water (approximately 1:18). Grind: Medium-coarse. Water: 93°C (±1°C). Vessel: Standardised cupping bowl (240ml). Multiple samples of the same coffee brewed simultaneously (typically 5 bowls) to identify inconsistency or defects. Coffees are labelled blind — no brand or origin information visible to maintain objectivity.

👃

The Sequence

1. Fragrance: Smell dry grounds before water is added. 2. Aroma: Smell immediately after hot water is added (wet crust). 3. Break: At 4 minutes, stir crust forward — lean in and inhale at the moment of breaking. 4. Clean up: Remove floating grounds and foam with two spoons. 5. Slurp: Using a cupping spoon, slurp coffee loudly (aerates it, spreads it across palate) and evaluate. Cool slightly between tastings.

What You're Evaluating

Fragrance / Aroma

Dry (fragrance) vs wet (aroma). Often the most immediately expressive phase — floral, fruit, chocolate, caramel, spice. Aromatics deteriorate fast; assess immediately.

Flavour

The combined taste and retronasal aroma experience on the palate. The main impression — fruit, sweetness, earthiness, etc. Evaluated at ~70°C.

Aftertaste

What lingers after swallowing. Good coffee: pleasant, long, evolving finish. Defective coffee: short, harsh, ashy, papery aftertaste.

Acidity

Not "sour" — quality acidity is brightness and liveliness. Like biting a ripe apple. Can be citric, malic, tartaric, phosphoric. Absent in dark roasts. Prominent in light roasts from high altitudes.

Body

Weight and mouthfeel — the viscosity and density of the liquid on the tongue. French press = heavy body. V60 paper filter = light body. Neither is "better." Context determines preference.

Balance

How well all attributes work together. A coffee can score high on individual attributes but low on balance if they conflict or one dominates inappropriately.

Sweetness

Natural sweetness from fruit sugars surviving processing and roasting. Distinct from added sugar. High-quality coffees have inherent sweetness — caramel, honey, fruit sugar. Under-developed or over-extracted coffee lacks sweetness.

Overall / Score

Total SCA score out of 100. 80+ = Specialty grade. 85+ = Outstanding. 90+ = Exceptional (rare, auction-priced). Below 80 = commercial grade. The cup score on your specialty bag refers to this system.

"The SCA Flavour Wheel has 110 descriptors. New tasters see them as impossible. Experienced tasters know they're just shorthand for things your nose already knows — they just need to be named."

Building Your Palate — A Practical Guide

🍋

Train Acidity

Eat fresh fruit daily and pay attention to the sourness. Ripe vs unripe mango, lime vs lemon, passion fruit vs orange. In coffee, citric acidity (lemon, orange) comes from Kenyan or Ethiopian coffees. Malic acidity (apple, grape) from Colombian or Central American. Phosphoric acidity (carbonated brightness) from high-altitude Kenyan.

🍫

Train Sweetness & Body

Compare dark chocolate (70%, 85%, 90%) for bitterness and sweetness. Compare honey varieties (acacia vs raw) for floral vs dark sweetness. Body in coffee maps to viscosity in everyday food — compare milk and water on your tongue. This translates directly to French press vs filtered coffee body.

🌿

Taste Comparatively

The most powerful way to build a palate: brew the same recipe with two different origins side by side. Ethiopian vs Colombian. Natural vs washed. You cannot "learn" the difference by description — only by tasting them next to each other. Keep notes. Date them. Return to them in 6 months and see how your description changes.

The Tools — Small Things That Change Everything

Small Tools, Massive Impact

The Details That Separate Good from Great

Precision Scale (0.1g)

The single most impactful accessory in coffee. Brewing by weight (not volume) is how you achieve repeatability. A 1g difference in dose can be the difference between perfection and disappointment in espresso.

Recs: Acaia Pearl · Timemore Black Mirror · Hario V60 Scale
Gooseneck Kettle

For pour-over brewing, a gooseneck spout gives precise, controlled water flow. A regular kettle pours too fast and too wide — you lose pour control entirely, creating channelling and uneven extraction.

Recs: Fellow Stagg EKG · Hario Buono · Brewista Smart Pour
Tamper (58mm)

For espresso — compresses the puck to create even resistance for water. Most commercial portafilter baskets are 58mm. Flat tamper preferred (even compression). The W340 from ONA Coffee (flat tamper with a specific convex edge) became famous after WBC usage. Even pressure matters — unlevel tamping causes channelling.

Recs: ONA W340 · Decent Tamper · Pullman BigStep · Normcore
WDT Tool

Weiss Distribution Technique. A tool with fine needles that stirs the ground coffee in the portafilter basket before tamping, breaking up clumps and ensuring even distribution. Eliminates "fluking" and channelling. A $20 tool that transforms espresso consistency dramatically.

Recs: Numerous options; DIY versions also highly effective
Distribution Tool / Leveler

Spins and levels the coffee grounds in the portafilter before tamping — ensures even bed density. Works in combination with WDT. The Stockfleth's Move (rotating finger technique) achieves the same result manually.

Recs: OCD (Ona Coffee Distribution) · MHW-3Bomber
Puck Screen

A thin stainless steel screen placed on top of the puck inside the portafilter. Distributes water evenly from the group head across the entire puck surface. Reduces channelling significantly. Simple, cheap, dramatically effective.

~₹500–1,500
Hario V60 (01 / 02)

The most culturally important pour-over dripper in the world. Japanese. Conical shape, single large hole, spiral ribs — requires pouring skill and consistency. Size 01 (1–2 cups), 02 (1–4 cups). Paper filters must be rinsed before use.

~₹800–2,500 depending on material
Refractometer (TDS meter)

Measures Total Dissolved Solids — how much coffee is actually dissolved in your cup. Allows you to calculate extraction yield precisely. The science tool that separates serious home brewers and professionals from casual users. Not needed to enjoy coffee. Essential to understand it.

Recs: Atago PAL-COFFEE · VST Refractometer

Season 10

What's Fake — The Myths, Scams & Shortcuts

❌ The 15-Bar Marketing Lie

Every budget espresso machine advertises "15 bar" or "19 bar" pump pressure as if more bars = better coffee. This is false. The SCA standard for espresso is 9 bars at the group head. Machines with 15-bar pumps either have an OPV (Over Pressure Valve) that reduces the pressure to ~9 bars at the puck (good) — or they genuinely push 15 bars through the coffee (bad, producing bitter, over-extracted espresso). The bar spec of the pump is meaningless. What matters is the pressure at the coffee puck. Always ask: does this machine have a pressure gauge? Can the OPV be adjusted?

❌ Pod Machines (Nespresso / Dolce Gusto)

Pod machines are convenient. They are not espresso. Real espresso requires: a correctly dialled-in burr grinder, fresh beans within 3 weeks of roast, a non-pressurised basket, and correctly calibrated pressure. A pod contains pre-ground coffee of unknown roast date sealed under pressure — it is, by definition, stale. The pressurised pod capsule also means the machine doesn't need to extract properly — the pod itself creates resistance artificially. The resulting drink is "espresso-style" at best. The environmental cost (billions of single-use capsules annually) is a separate catastrophe. Nespresso's crema is largely produced by pressurised gas infusion in the capsule — not actual extraction quality.

❌ Instant Coffee as "Coffee"

Instant coffee is brewed coffee that has been either spray-dried (liquid coffee sprayed into a hot chamber until it becomes powder) or freeze-dried (frozen into ice then sublimated into powder). What you are re-dissolving is pre-brewed, thermally degraded coffee. The volatile aromatics — the reason coffee smells and tastes complex — are mostly destroyed during the spray-drying process. What remains is a caffeine delivery device with a simplified, flat flavour profile. Freeze-dried is marginally better. Neither is coffee as specialty defines it. In India: Nescafé Gold, Bru Green Label — sold as convenience, not as quality.

❌ Superautomatic Machines (One-Button Everything)

Bean-to-cup superautomatics (DeLonghi Magnifica, Jura, Melitta) grind and brew in one machine at the press of a button. Convenient. Not specialty-grade. The built-in grinders are typically conical burr but designed for speed and durability, not precision. The brewing chamber pre-heats coffee to extreme temperatures. The grind-to-brew time is near-zero (no settling, no WDT, no distribution). The result is convenient but inconsistent — you cannot dial it in the way you can a separate grinder and machine. Fine for an office. Not fine for a café or a serious home bar.

❌ "Espresso Roast" as a Label

"Espresso roast" is not a roast level. It is a marketing term. Any roast level can be brewed as espresso. A light roast Ethiopian can be pulled as espresso (sour, fruity, intense). A dark roast blend can be pulled as espresso (bitter, body-heavy, classic). "Espresso roast" on a bag means the roaster thinks the beans work well for espresso — it does not mean dark roast, it does not mean better, and it does not legally mean anything at all.
